England by Class — a film syllabus

A third cut of the England shelf. The chronological course asks what happened next; England by Its Invaders asks who is arriving; this one asks who owns it, and who works it. ⭐ The library answers that question lopsidedly, and the syllabus is built to show the lopsidedness rather than smooth it. Measured 2026-10-14 against a canon of 67 titles: the satires of the ruling class are 7 of 11 held, the country house 5 of 9 — and the British New Wave of 1958–63, the decade English cinema discovered the working class, is 0 of 10. Session 16 is therefore empty on purpose. ⚠️ A tag search cannot build this course: `class` is split across fifteen near-synonyms and the 93 films carrying one include only 14 from England, every one of them a period costume drama. Brassed Off, Billy Elliot and This Is England carry no class tag at all.

I · The Land weeks 1–4 · 15 films

Week 1

Villein and Lord

Before class is an argument it is an arrangement about land. Labour service, the manor court, and then the plague — which killed enough people that the survivors could ask for wages and be legislated against for asking. ⭐ The Statute of Labourers (1351) is the first English law passed to hold wages DOWN; the shelf has no film of it, so it has to be read off the two Kingsbridge serials.

Week 2

1381 and 1649: When the Bottom Spoke

Two risings that stated a programme rather than a grievance. Tyler at Smithfield wanted the end of villeinage; Winstanley wanted the end of property, and went and dug on St George’s Hill to prove it. ⭐ Winstanley (1975) was shot by Kevin Brownlow over five years with amateurs in real weather, which is itself a statement about who gets to be filmed.

Week 3

The Estate and the Marriage Market

The polite version of the same arrangement. In Austen the entail is the plot — property moves by inheritance and marriage, and a woman’s whole economic life is the negotiation. ⚠️ These are the films a `class` tag search DOES return, which is exactly the problem: the costume drama is over-tagged for class and the factory floor is not tagged at all.

Hardy’s Labourers

The one 19th-century novelist who wrote the agricultural working class from inside it. Tess is a dairymaid destroyed by the respectability of people with money; Jude is refused the university by birth; Henchard sells his wife at a fair and spends the book paying for it.

II · The Mill weeks 5–8 · 19 films

Week 5

Peterloo and the Unreformed Parliament

16 August 1819: cavalry ride into a reform meeting at St Peter’s Field and kill eighteen people. Leigh films it as four hours of people TALKING — committees, drafting, rehearsing the speech — because the argument is that the crowd was organised, literate and political, which is what frightened the magistrates.

Week 7

Dickens and the Poor Law

Eight adaptations, one argument: the 1834 New Poor Law made destitution a punishable condition, and Dickens spent a career on the institutions it built — the workhouse, the debtors’ prison, the Chancery suit that eats an inheritance, the charity school. ⭐ These sit on Literature/Dickens, not England: filed by author, which is why no England-shelf browse has ever shown them as social evidence.

Week 8

The Pit, Told Elsewhere

⭐ This is the session where the hole shows. The industrial working class at its most filmable — the coalfield, the union, the strike — and the library tells it in Welsh, American and French. How Green Was My Valley is a Welsh valley shot in California; Matewan and The Molly Maguires are Pennsylvania; Salt of the Earth was made by blacklisted Americans and banned; Germinal is Zola. England’s own version of this story is session 16, and it is empty.

Week 12

The Ladder, and Who Is Kept Off It

⭐ Kes is the whole course in ninety minutes. A Barnsley boy with no prospects trains a kestrel, and the careers master gives him ninety seconds and writes down “manual”. Against it, Chariots of Fire — where the ladder exists, is in Cambridge, and is still rationed by birth and religion.

V · The Joke weeks 14–15 · 6 films

Week 14

Inheritance as Farce

When the arrangement can no longer be defended it gets laughed at. A draughtsman is hired, used and disposed of by his betters; a disinherited heir murders eight D’Ascoynes, all played by Alec Guinness; and the 14th Earl of Gurney believes he is God, is declared cured once he begins behaving like Jack the Ripper, and is welcomed to the House of Lords on exactly that basis.

Week 15

The Mick Travis Trilogy

⭐ Held complete, and nothing in the library said so. Lindsay Anderson and Malcolm McDowell made three films across fourteen years with the same character’s name: the public school as a firing range (1968), the coffee salesman loose in a corrupt Britain (1973), and the hospital as the whole country (1982). All three sit unremarked on the unshelved Movies folder, on no syllabus until this one.

VI · From Below weeks 16–19 · 12 films

Week 16

The Kitchen Sink — the decade the library does not have

⛔ This session has no films, and that is the finding. Between 1958 and 1963 English cinema discovered the working class: Richardson, Reisz, Schlesinger, Anderson, the Woodfall films, working-class protagonists in their own accents in real towns. The library holds none of the ten. A fuzzy match at 0.80 against every title AND original title returns nothing close for all of them. Set against 7 of 11 ruling-class satires and 5 of 9 country-house films, the shape is not bad luck — the collection can watch the ruling class being laughed at and cannot watch anybody working. Everything below is WANTED.

  • Room at the Top 1959movienot heldClayton from John Braine. The film that opened the cycle and took the X certificate seriously — Joe Lampton calculating his way up out of Dufton. The single most load-bearing absence on this syllabus.
  • Saturday Night and Sunday Morning 1960movienot heldReisz from Sillitoe, Albert Finney at a Nottingham lathe. "All I want is a good time. The rest is propaganda."
  • A Taste of Honey 1961movienot heldRichardson from Shelagh Delaney, who wrote the play at eighteen. Salford, a pregnant schoolgirl, a black sailor and a gay friend — the one film of the cycle not centred on an angry young man.
  • The Loneliness of the Long Distance Runner 1962movienot heldRichardson from Sillitoe again: a borstal boy is trained to win a race for the governor and throws it at the line. Would pair directly with Kes in session 12.
  • This Sporting Life 1963movienot heldLindsay Anderson’s first feature, Richard Harris as a rugby league miner. ⭐ Its absence is why session 15 begins at 1968 — the library holds Anderson’s satires and not his realism.
  • A Kind of Loving 1962movienot heldSchlesinger’s first feature, from Stan Barstow. A draughtsman, a shotgun marriage and a mother-in-law in a northern semi.
  • Billy Liar 1963movienot heldSchlesinger from Waterhouse. The undertaker’s clerk who will not get on the train — the cycle’s own verdict on escape.
  • Look Back in Anger 1959movienot heldRichardson from Osborne, Burton as Jimmy Porter. The play that named the Angry Young Men and started the whole thing in 1956.
  • The Entertainer 1960movienot heldOsborne and Richardson again, Olivier as a failing music-hall comic — the Empire and the variety bill going down together. Would sit beside Topsy-Turvy in session 13.
  • Poor Cow 1967movienot heldKen Loach’s first feature, and the hinge between the New Wave and the realist line this course picks up in session 19.
Week 17

The Strike

1984–85 from three angles, all made a decade or more after it ended. Grimethorpe’s own colliery band plays itself in Brassed Off; Pride is the London lesbians and gay men who twinned with a Welsh mining village and the village that marched for them afterwards; Billy Elliot puts the pickets in the background of a ballet lesson and lets you work out the cost.

The Line, and the Women On It

The sewing machinists at Ford Dagenham strike in 1968 and the Equal Pay Act follows in 1970; the suffrage campaign is told, unusually, through a laundry worker rather than a Pankhurst; Loach takes the same argument to Los Angeles janitors. ⚠️ Only one of the three is on the England shelf.

Quadrophenia is 1964 looked at from 1979; A Clockwork Orange is 1971 looking FORWARD at an underclass the welfare state has not absorbed, and is in this term rather than with the satires for that reason; I, Daniel Blake is the Poor Law of session 7 rebuilt as a telephone queue.

No Footage Exists — history’s unfilmed catastrophes and the fictions that stand in for them

A library of history on film has a hole exactly where the century’s biggest fears are, and the hole has a cause: the things most feared did not happen, so nobody filmed them. There is no documentary of a continent destroyed by nuclear war or a planet emptied by plague. The only record of how 1984 felt about the first is Threads. ⭐ And this library already agreed, once, and never noticed: the Cold War shelf holds seven works of science fiction — Animal Farm, On the Beach, Colossus, WarGames, The Day After, Threads and the explicitly counterfactual For All Mankind — filed as history without anyone flinching. Nineteen SF titles sit on the four war shelves. Ninety-five sit unshelved. The precedent exists; it was never generalised. ⚠️ The course is built in pairs: a regime that existed, then the fiction that displaced it. Alfred’s framing, 2026-10-24: "a lot of scifi is social and technological what-iffing … the kaiju movies like godzilla were a catharsis/way of reckoning with the 2 bombs." ⛔ Two sessions are nearly empty on purpose — the library has no Godzilla at all, and neither of Peter Watkins’ two essential films.

Week 5

Orwell, Three Times, and the Broadcast That Became an Event

⭐ The 1954 BBC Nineteen Eighty-Four is not an adaptation of history, it IS one. Broadcast live on 12 December 1954, it drew an unprecedented volume of viewer complaints, questions in Parliament and several Early Day Motions — one deploring the BBC’s "tendency … to pander to sexual and sadistic tastes". The BBC ran the repeat anyway and it took the largest audience since the Coronation; the repeat was recorded because of the row, which is the only reason the production survives to be watched now. ⛔ And Animal Farm (1954), beside it on the Cold War shelf, was covertly funded by the CIA, which bought the rights from Orwell’s widow and argued the animators out of Orwell’s ending. Two Orwell adaptations in one year, one a scandal and one an operation.

THE ADAPTATION BECOMES THE EVENT. The BBC Nineteen Eighty-Four went out live on Sunday 12 December 1954 and produced an unprecedented volume of viewer complaints, questions in Parliament and several Early Day Motions, one deploring the Corporation's "tendency … to pander to sexual and sadistic tastes". The BBC transmitted the Thursday repeat anyway; it took the largest television audience since the Coronation. ⭐ And the repeat was recorded BECAUSE of the row — which is the only reason the production survives to be watched now. A film about the rewriting of the record exists only as the copy the scandal caused to be kept. ⛔ Beside it on the Cold War shelf, Animal Farm (1954) was covertly financed by the CIA, through the Office of Policy Coordination using Louis de Rochemont's company as a front; the rights had been bought from Orwell's widow in 1950. The animators were argued out of Orwell's ending, in which the pigs and the men are indistinguishable. ⚠ Sources differ on the share ("in part" against "almost entirely") and Vivien Halas, the directors' daughter, says her parents did not know. Week 9

Peter Watkins, and the Fiction Shot As Fact

⭐ The library holds four Watkins films and files them on four different shelves, so the method has never been visible as a method. He invented one form — the event filmed as television news, handheld, with vox pops and a reporter who gets in the way — and then aimed it at an eighteenth-century battle (Culloden, 1964, tagged `docudrama` on Scotland), a painter (Edvard Munch, on Biopic), an American near-future (Punishment Park, tagged `mockumentary`, unshelved) and the Paris Commune at five and three-quarter hours with non-professional actors and two invented TV channels. ⛔ The one he is most famous for is the one missing: The War Game (1966), commissioned by the BBC, judged too horrifying to transmit, shelved for twenty years, and winning an Oscar from a cinema release it was never meant to have. It is the film Threads is answering in session 12.

THE TWO FILMS WERE THE SAME LAWSUIT. Peter George, who wrote Red Alert, sued the authors of Fail-Safe, their publishers and the production company in February 1963 for plagiarism and unfair competition; Kubrick's companies and Columbia joined the filing. ⭐ The settlement dissolved the rival production outright and Columbia took over Fail Safe — so one studio now owned both films, and Kubrick insisted his come out first. Dr. Strangelove opened in January 1964 and Fail Safe eight months later, to a fraction of the attention: the comedy buried the tragedy, by contract. ⚠ Sources differ on exactly who the plaintiffs were. ⭐ Note also that the library files Fail Safe on Russia — defensible, since the target is Moscow, but it means no Cold War browse has ever shown it beside the film it is the straight version of.

Week 12

The Aftermath, And Who Was Made to Watch It

⭐ The strongest case in the course. The Day After (1983) was watched by something near half the adult United States on one night; Threads (1984) answered it from Sheffield with thirteen years of consequences and no music. These are not films about the future — they are the primary source for how 1983 felt, and nothing else is. ⚠️ All four are already shelved as Cold War history. Nobody had to be persuaded.

THE LARGEST AUDIENCE ANY OF THESE FILMS WILL EVER HAVE. The Day After went out on ABC on 20 November 1983 to roughly 100 million American viewers — a 62% share of sets in use, in a country of 234 million. ABC had struggled to sell the advertising, letting spots go for as little as $11,000. Reagan was shown it before transmission and wrote in his diary that it was "very effective" and had left him greatly depressed; ⚠ biographers differ on how far to connect that to the INF Treaty four years later, and Edmund Morris's version is speculation, not record. ⭐ Threads answered it from Sheffield ten months later with thirteen years of consequences, no score and a closing freeze-frame — and Threads is itself answering The War Game, which the BBC had refused to show (session 9). The chain is American network television to British public broadcasting to a film the same broadcaster had suppressed, in nineteen years.

Week 13

Gojira, 1954 — And Why This Session Is Nearly Empty

⛔ The library owns no Godzilla. Its only monster films are King Kong (1933), tagged `dinosaur`, Frankenstein and The Thing. ⚠️ And the usual summary — catharsis for Hiroshima and Nagasaki — understates it by one event and nine years. Gojira opened on 3 November 1954, eight months after the Castle Bravo test irradiated the crew of the Daigo Fukuryū Maru; the producer took the idea from that news story. The film opens on a ship destroyed by a flash of light at sea, and the life preserver reads "No. 5" — the Lucky Dragon’s number, without naming it. It is not a retrospective about 1945. It is a current-affairs film wearing a rubber suit, and it was in cinemas while the boat’s radio operator was still dying.

THE COUNTERFACTUAL USED TO TEACH AN ACTUAL EVENT. Watchmen (2019) opens on the Tulsa race massacre of 31 May–1 June 1921, staged at length and without comment, before any of its invented history begins — and a large part of its American audience had never been taught that it happened. ⭐ A series about masked vigilantes in a world where Nixon served five terms is the vehicle by which a real massacre reached a mass audience. Sankofa does the inverse: it sends a present-day fashion model bodily back into slavery because the costume-drama frame would not carry it. ⭐ And all three films in this session are already filed as history on this library's shelves — For All Mankind, which is explicitly counterfactual, sits on Cold War beside Threads and The Day After.

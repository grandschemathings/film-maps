History of Spain — a film syllabus

Three terms of eight. Shorter than the other courses on purpose: the Spain shelf holds 40 items against England’s 146 and France’s 218, and padding a thin shelf would misrepresent it. Two things are distinctive here. The Reconquista and the Golden Age are carried almost entirely by SERIES rather than features; and the Conquest of the Americas - Spain’s largest single historical subject - sits on the MesoAmerica-1492 shelf, because this library files by where a film is SET.

Week 1

Al-Andalus

Eight centuries of Muslim Iberia, and the convivencia argument that still runs.

  • Réquiem por Granada 1991 seriesSpain/SeriesRVW
  • The Castilian 1963 Spain/MoviesRVW

Jerónimo in The Castilian is a peasant from the mountains of Oca who fights beside Fernán González — an ordinary name, which is the point. Roughly nine centuries later and half a world away, Mexican soldiers in the Sierra Madre gave that same name to an Apache leader, and Goyaałé became Geronimo. ⚠ The familiar story — that they were calling on Saint Jerome as he charged — is DISPUTED: others hold it was simply a Mexican nickname, or their mangling of Goyaałé, and the saint invoked may have been Jerome Emiliani (1486–1537) rather than Jerome of Stridon. What is not in doubt is that the name is the Spanish form of Jerome, and that the Mexicans conferred it rather than the Apache.

Week 2

El Cid and the Reconquista

A mercenary who fought for both sides, remade as the national hero.

  • El Cid 1961 Spain/MoviesRVW
  • The Legend of El Cid 2020 seriesSpain/SeriesRVW

FORWARD POINTER, to the explorers. The surname Cabeza de Vaca — literally “cow’s head” — is traditionally explained by Las Navas de Tolosa, July 1212: a shepherd, Martín Alhaja, is said to have marked an unguarded mountain pass with a cow’s skull so the Christian army could take the Almohads from behind, and to have been ennobled with the name. Álvar Núñez took it from his MOTHER’S line — a matronymic, unusual enough to notice on its own. Three centuries later he walked from the Gulf of Mexico to the Pacific over eight years; the library holds Nicolás Echevarría’s Cabeza de Vaca (1991), filed with the Conquest on the MesoAmerica shelf. ⚠ MODERN SCHOLARSHIP REJECTS the Alhaja story — the true origin of the name is unknown and the line is traced instead to a 13th-century nobleman, Pérez Cabeza de Vaca. Keep the legend anyway: it is the better teaching object. A family carried a Reconquista founding-myth in its surname, sent a son to America, and the myth crossed with him. The New York Times was still printing it as fact in 1930.

Who Belongs — a film syllabus on citizenship, migration and the state that decides

A course built on one claim: belonging is not a feeling, it is a status — granted, withheld, inherited, forged, bought, or revoked by somebody with a desk. The films are therefore chosen for the MOMENT THE STATUS IS DECIDED rather than for the migration story around it: a licence clerk reading a code number, an Ellis Island quarantine, a boarding party counting men on a deck, a judge asking whether a cargo is people. ⭐ It opens with the reverse term, at Alfred’s instruction. Term I is impressment — the case where a state refuses to let you RESIGN a nationality, which is the mirror image of every later session, where a state refuses to GRANT one. Britain’s doctrine was indefeasible allegiance, nemo potest exuere patriam: once a subject, always a subject, naturalisation elsewhere being legally meaningless. The United States had just built a country on the opposite premise. Mutiny (1952) states the whole conflict in two lines of dialogue in its first two minutes, and the War of 1812 follows from it. ⚠️ Built from measurement, not from memory. Every nfo in the library was swept for a 49-term vocabulary (immigrant, refugee, deportation, naturalisation, statelessness, exile, repatriation, impressment and the rest), and the tag crop was then probed against a canon list of 200 titles — because a tag sweep alone cannot find a film whose nfo never names the subject. 84 of those 200 are held. ⚠️ The two tags are a split, not a pair: immigrant (23 films) and immigration (7) share only El Norte, so the library is already describing the same thing two ways. ⭐ Several claims here were checked against the SUBTITLE TRACK rather than a synopsis, and the sessions say so where it matters.

I · The Reverse Term: Allegiance You Cannot Resign weeks 1–3 · 10 films

Week 1

Impressment: “Aye, but I’m an American”

⭐ THE REVERSE CASE, AND THE COURSE STARTS HERE ON PURPOSE. Every other session is a person trying to get in and a state saying no. This one is a state saying you never left. A British boarding party reads a name off a muster and asks a single question — You were born in Liverpool, weren’t you? — and the answer, Aye, but I’m an American, is legally worthless under the doctrine of indefeasible allegiance. Verified in the first ninety seconds of Mutiny’s subtitle track, not from a synopsis.

  • Billy Budd 1962 MoviesQ1121916RVW
  • H.M.S. Defiant 1962 England/MoviesRVW
  • Master and Commander: The Far Side of the World 2003 England/MoviesRVW
  • The Impressment of Seamen anynot heldNo documentary held on impressment itself. The practice is dramatised in Mutiny (1952) and nowhere explained.

⭐ The doctrine has a name, and the war that follows is in this library twice. Britain held to indefeasible allegiance — nemo potest exuere patriam, no one may cast off his fatherland — so a Liverpool-born sailor carrying American naturalisation papers was, to a Royal Navy boarding officer, simply a British subject who had been somewhere. Between 1793 and 1812 something like 6,000 to 9,000 men were taken off American ships on that reasoning. The United States, a country four years older than the practice was contested, held the opposite: that allegiance is chosen and transferable. ⭐ That is not a background detail, it is a stated war aim. Mutiny (1952) names 18 June 1812 in its third minute and puts the whole argument in a boarding scene before the credits are over: As Master of this ship, I’ll not stand for the impressment of American seamen · Edward Jones. You were born in Liverpool, weren’t you? · Aye, but I’m an American. ⭐ And the course connects straight into the presidency course here. Session 4 of The American Presidency is Madison and 1812, and Magnificent Doll (1946) — the Dolley Madison film — now carries P180 depicts for the War of 1812 and the Burning of Washington on Wikidata, filed 16 October 2026. The impressment session and that session are the same history approached from opposite ends: one from a deck, one from a drawing room. ⚠️ The legal story does not end until 1870, when Britain finally conceded the point in the Naturalisation Act — which means the doctrine outlived the war it caused by fifty-eight years.

Week 3

Citizenship Through a Husband

⭐ The other reverse term, and it is in a quiet Minnesota film nobody files here. A German bride reaches a licence window in 1920 and is read a code number: Women are considered chattel of the husband or father, and it is assumed that citizenship is the same as the husband or father — then I don’t know who this woman is without the correct papers. That is derivative citizenship, and under the 1907 Expatriation Act it ran the other way too: an American woman who married a foreigner lost her own nationality. ⚠️ Sweet Land carries both the citizenship and german american tags and is the ONLY film in the library tagged either.

  • Sweet Land 2005 Westerns/MoviesQ7655387RVW
  • The Europeans 1979 MoviesQ1405838RVW
  • I Remember Mama 1948 MoviesQ1498445RVW

⭐ Derivative citizenship is the most invisible rule in the whole course, and one farm film has it exactly right. The clerk in Sweet Land is not improvising; he reads from a code. Under the Expatriation Act of 1907 an American woman who married a foreign man took his nationality and lost her own — she did not have to leave the country, or even the county, to stop being a citizen. It was partially repaired by the Cable Act of 1922 and not fully until 1940. ⚠️ So a woman could be made stateless by a wedding, and the film’s refused licence is the same machinery seen from the other side. ⭐ Alongside it, the 1917 Immigration Act’s literacy test and the wartime suspicion of Germans are both on screen — or something worse, a lousy German — which is why this sits in Term I with the impressment films rather than in the arrival sessions. All three are cases of a status assigned TO a person rather than claimed BY one.

IV · Chinese in the West weeks 12–13 · 9 films

Week 12

Chinese in the West

⭐ ALFRED’S SET, and it is bigger than it looks — the library holds five Westerns with Chinese protagonists or partners. First Cow is the quiet one and the best: I’m Chinese · Chinese in these parts, a Chinese drifter and a Jewish-American cook inventing a business in 1820s Oregon. Thousand Pieces of Gold is the hard one — a woman sold into an Idaho saloon, with your citizenship papers and I’m Hong King’s slave in the same script, which is the Page Act of 1875 and the Exclusion Act of 1882 dramatised in one life.

  • First Cow 2020 Westerns/MoviesQ65082453RVW
  • Thousand Pieces of Gold 1991 Westerns/MoviesQ7797064RVW
  • Shanghai Noon 2000 Westerns/MoviesQ849475RVW
  • Shanghai Knights 2003 Westerns/MoviesQ595595RVW
  • Once Upon a Time in China and America 1997 Westerns/MoviesQ691478RVW

⭐ The legal spine of this session is two statutes, and both are visible on screen. The Page Act of 1875 barred the entry of Chinese women on the presumption that they were being imported for prostitution — which is precisely the transaction Thousand Pieces of Gold opens with, and the film keeps the vocabulary: Hong King’s Chinese girl · I’m Hong King’s slave · I don’t want any slave money. The Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 then made Chinese labourers the first nationality in American history barred by name, and made naturalisation impossible for those already present — so your citizenship papers, spoken in an Idaho mining camp, is a line about something the law had just put out of reach. ⚠️ It was not repealed until 1943, and then with a quota of 105 people a year. ⭐ The tag is real but thin: chinese immigrant is on seven films, which is one of the better-populated subject tags in this whole course — compare citizenship, which is on exactly one. ⚠️ Ip Man 4 (1960s San Francisco) and Year of the Dragon (1980s New York) are the same subject a century later and carry no shared tag with the Westerns at all, which is why no query would ever have assembled this session.

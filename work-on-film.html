Work on Film

A claim from a film forum: films and shows were about workers until Norma Rae; new ones are all about bankers, managers and billionaires. Tested against a private collection of 4,135 films and 267 series. ⭐ The claim is roughly right and its hinge is wrong — there are four phases, not two, and the worker does not vanish in 1979. He changes desks, and then the camera changes floors.

4,135 films 267 series 4 phases 3 controlled pairs 2 complete

What this collection can and cannot settle. It is a curated history library with a roughly 25-year subject rule and a 61% American tilt. It is not a sample of cinema and cannot test what gets made. It can test what is in it — and it can be surprised, which is the useful part. Every claim below names its denominator; where a finding could be an artefact of how the collection was built, it says so.

1Four phases

Each phase believes in a different remedy. That is what actually changes.

PhaseWindowWhat the film thinks can be done

Phase 1 ends with the plants, not with Norma Rae

Blue Collar (1978), F.I.S.T. (1978), The Deer Hunter (1978), Harlan County U.S.A. (1977). The collection's own 1970s peak — the highest labour rate of any decade — is overwhelmingly male and extractive: Kes, The Molly Maguires, Joe Hill, Bound for Glory, Alambrista!, Days of Heaven, Breaking Away.

Phase 2 belongs to a different movement

Norma Rae, 9 to 5 and Working Girl are not in that cluster. They are downstream of women's liberation and the Equal Rights Amendment, which passed Congress in 1972, reached its deadline in March 1979 and its extension in June 1982. Norma Rae is 1979. 9 to 5 is 1980. By Working Girl in 1988 the amendment has been dead six years and the film's resolution is a promotion.

⭐ And the collective drains out inside the phase.

  • Norma Rae (1979) — a real union drive: ACTWU at J.P. Stevens, from Crystal Lee Sutton.
  • 9 to 5 (1980) — sex discrimination, via the 9to5 organisation of working women. Not a union.
  • Working Girl (1988) — one woman gets a corner office. No collective at all.

So Working Girl is already a capital film in sympathy — which is exactly why it pairs with Money Never Sleeps so cleanly.

Phase 3 is the one the instrument could not see

Found only because someone remembered Office Space (1999). It does not stand alone: the collection already holds three of its cohort — Glengarry Glen Ross (1992), Falling Down (1993), Clerks (1994) — and this accounts for a 1990s figure that otherwise made no sense, 3.7 per 100, nearly the 1970s rate, which had been misread as a late labour revival.

⛔ The keyword buckets missed it completely, because its vocabulary is bad boss, downsizing, burnout, corporate world — not union and strike. The hole in the instrument was exactly the width of the film that was remembered.

Phase 4 is bigger than the films suggest

Finance-system keywords: 2 films before 2000, 14 after. The wall street keyword is on 8 films and all 8 are 2000 or later. The kind changes with the count — before 2000 a money film is about a rich man (Citizen Kane, The Great Gatsby, The Thomas Crown Affair); after, about a mechanism, with barely a protagonist.

⭐ And nine capital-side series, every one released 2014 or later: Halt and Catch Fire, McMafia, The Loudest Voice, Valley of the Boom, Industry, ZeroZeroZero, Super Pumped, Painkiller, Palm Royale. Including television makes the shift sharper, not softer.

3Three controlled pairs

Fix one variable, move the date. This is the argument; everything above is scaffolding.

Fix the director

The Molly Maguires (1970) · held  /  Norma Rae (1979) · held

Martin Ritt directed both. The same man made the male industrial labour film and the female one, nine years apart. Verified from the collection's own metadata.

Fix the genre ⭐ the best of the three

9 to 5 (1980) · held  /  Office Space (1999) · held

Identical genre — the office comedy about hating your boss — nineteen years apart, and only the remedy moves. In 1980 three women organise and win daycare, flexitime and equal pay. In 1999 one man stops showing up and his friends commit fraud.

And Office Space ends with Peter leaving the office for construction work, happier — phase 1 surviving as nostalgia inside phase 3. It reads as a counterexample and works as a confirmation: the workers are still on screen. What has gone is any belief that something could be done together.

Fix the street

Working Girl (1988) · held  /  Wall Street: Money Never Sleeps (2010) · wanted

Same street, same industry, twenty-two years apart. In 1988 the camera is on the secretary riding the ferry in; in 2010 it is on the men she brought coffee to. Nothing changed but whose job the film thinks is the story. A better test than Norma Rae against The Big Short, because the setting is held fixed.

⭐ Two of the three pairs are now complete, acquired against this page on 24 October 2026 — Norma Rae, 9 to 5, Office Space and Working Girl arrived together, along with North Country, Blue Collar and Clockwatchers. Only the street is still half: Money Never Sleeps is the one film left. ⚠️ And Wall Street (1987) itself is not held, although the collection now carries nine films tagged wall street.

5What the argument asked for, and what arrived

The seven titles this section listed as missing were acquired on 24 October 2026, in rank order. They are kept here because the list is the argument’s own record of what it needed before it had it.

  1. North Country (2005) · held — the first sexual-harassment class action in US law, set in an iron mine, the heroine’s father on the same shop floor. ⭐ The only film that puts phase 2 physically inside phase 1.
  2. Office Space (1999) · held — the phase-3 keystone, and half of the best pair.
  3. Norma Rae (1979) · held — completes the Ritt pair.
  4. Working Girl (1988) and 9 to 5 (1980) · held — the other pair halves.
  5. Blue Collar (1978) and Clockwatchers (1997) · held — the end of phase 1 and the overlooked link between 9 to 5 and Office Space.

⭐ And watching 9 to 5 changed the argument. At 1:44:13 of a 1:49 film — the last scene — the chairman of the board congratulates the boss on the reforms the three women installed in his name: the day care centre, “the cost was minimal … it’s cut down on absenteeism.” Then: “That equal pay thing, though, that’s, uh, got to go. It’s a good incentive, but we don’t need to keep on priming the pump.” Every reform that saved the company money is kept; the one that costs money is cancelled — in a comedy remembered as a victory. No plot summary mentions it. The film’s own catalogue entry was 112 characters long.

Thirty-four titles are tracked in all, ranked by what the argument needs rather than by preference, and matched by database id rather than by title.

6How this was measured, and four ways it went wrong

Keeping the errors is the point: all four are the same failure, and it is the characteristic failure of asking a catalogue a question.

  1. “No Errol Flynn in the collection.” The query filtered on an Actors column the export does not have. The reader returns null, the filter matched nothing, and 4,121 rows produced a confident zero. There are 25 Flynn films and 19 Niven.
  2. “Mrs. America is not held.” A movies-only export asked a question about television. It is held — and it is the Equal Rights Amendment film, the exact gap that had just been declared.
  3. A plot-text pattern over series returned Rome, Kaamelott and Barbarians, because empire and fortune are period vocabulary. Discarded.
  4. The keyword buckets could not see phase 3 at all — see above.

⭐ Every one returned zero instead of an error, and zero looks like a finding. A missing column, the wrong table, a false-friend vocabulary and a missing vocabulary are indistinguishable from a true absence at the point of reading the output.

The three guards now in use: assert the field exists before filtering on it; name the denominator in the claim (“0 of 4,121 films” is checkable and exposes that 267 series were never looked at — “not held” is not); and ship a positive control, because a tool reporting “0 found” is otherwise indistinguishable from a broken one.

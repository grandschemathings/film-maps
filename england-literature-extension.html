English Literature on Film — the optional extension

An optional extension to History of England.

An optional extension to the History of England. The main course treats films as evidence about the past; this one follows the BOOKS. It exists because of a measurement: 585 films here are tagged "based on novel or book" and 570 of them are NOT on the Literature shelf. That shelf holds 75 items - Shakespeare and Dickens - because a shelf requires a physical move, which is why only two authors have one. Austen’s adaptations are scattered across SEVEN shelves; no shelf can hold her, but a syllabus can.

Session 1

Chaucer

The syllabus starts where English literature does, and with a twist: in both films Chaucer is a CHARACTER, not just the source. Pasolini keeps cutting back to him at his desk, played by Pasolini himself, so the tales are what the poet is imagining; A Knight’s Tale gives him to Paul Bettany as a broke, compulsive gambler who talks his way into forging a peasant’s patent of nobility — a herald, which is roughly what Chaucer’s day job was. ⭐ Neither film is English. Pasolini’s I racconti di Canterbury is an Italian–French co-production, in Italian and English, of the founding text of English poetry, and it took the Golden Bear at Berlin in 1972; A Knight’s Tale is American and was shot in Prague. The first week of an English literature course has no English production in it. Both are transpositions before the fact — Pasolini films the bawdy tales and leaves the piety out, Helgeland scores a joust with Queen — which is the move session 4 comes back to with Shakespeare. ⭐ The Green Knight is here as Chaucer’s CONTEMPORARY, not as his work: Sir Gawain and the Green Knight is anonymous, by the poet we call the Pearl Poet, written in the alliterative North-West Midlands dialect within a few years of the Tales and in a completely different English. Two poems, one decade, and only one of them is the line that modern English descends from. It is also Arthurian, and the shelf tags it that way — `king arthur`, `camelot`, `knights of the round table`. Both readings are true at once, and the lesson is that a film carries more than one axis: the legend it retells and the text that retells it are different facts about the same film, and a syllabus can hold both where a shelf has to pick one.

Session 2

The Henriad

Eight plays, one story, and the shelf can run the whole thing twice. In its larger sense the Henriad is not the four plays of Prince Hal but all eight, Richard II through Richard III, covering 1398 to 1485. The word is Alvin Kernan’s, coined in 1969 for the tetralogy; it is E. M. W. Tillyard’s 1944 framing that makes the eight read as one work. ⭐ The fact to open on: Shakespeare wrote it BACKWARDS. The three Henry VIs and Richard III came first, around 1591-94, and they cover the END of the story, 1422 to 1485; Richard II through Henry V came second, around 1595-99, and cover its BEGINNING, 1398 to 1415. That is why the ‘first tetralogy’ is the later history and the ‘second’ the earlier — the cycle was assembled as a prequel, by a writer who already knew how it ended. ⭐ Two complete cycles are held. The Hollow Crown does all eight in seven episodes across its two series (2012: Richard II, Henry IV 1 and 2, Henry V; 2016: Henry VI compressed into two, then Richard III), and the BBC Television Shakespeare of session 5 does them unabridged, scattered across its run at S01E02, S02E01-03 and S05E03-06. ⭐ Then the single plays, which is where the week earns its airtime. Three Henry Vs: Olivier 1944, Branagh 1989, and The King (2019), which is Henry IV and V loosely and sits on the Shakespeare shelf on that footing. ⚠️ Olivier’s opens in 1600 at the Globe — a playbill, a model of Elizabethan London, and the Chorus apologising for the wooden O — before dissolving out of the theatre into France; it is the only one of the three that stages the apology literally. (Checked on the held copy, which is the 1978 Rank/Granada reissue and carries no dedication card.) Two Richard IIIs: Olivier 1955, and Loncraine and McKellen’s 1995, which moves it to a fascist Britain of the 1930s and so belongs to session 4 as much as here. ⭐ Chimes at Midnight is the cycle re-cut around its minor character: Welles builds one film out of Richard II, both Henry IVs, Henry V and The Merry Wives of Windsor, and puts Falstaff at the centre, so the rejection becomes the ending rather than an incident. ⭐ The week closes on the afterlife, with two films about looking for the same king — Pacino hunting the character in Looking for Richard, and Philippa Langley hunting the body under a Leicester car park in The Lost King. ⚠️ The Lost King is NOT an adaptation and carries none of this week’s tags: its subject is the damage Shakespeare’s portrait did to the man.

Session 4

Shakespeare Transposed

What survives when the words go. ⭐ The week divides into three. First the transpositions that keep the plot and throw the language away: Kurosawa twice (Throne of Blood is Macbeth, Ran is Lear), West Side Story, and then the ones that relocate a play into a world small enough to make the ambition look absurd — Men Of Respect puts Macbeth in the New York mob, Scotland, PA puts him behind the counter of a 1970s Pennsylvania burger stand, and Maqbool puts him in the Mumbai underworld. ⓘ Count them: with Throne of Blood that is FOUR Macbeths in this session alone, which makes it comfortably the most transposed play on the shelf — the plot is portable because ambition and a murder scale to any size of kingdom, including a burger stand. ⭐ Second, the schoolroom relocations, which are their own genre and all arrive within seven years: 10 Things I Hate About You (The Taming of the Shrew), O (Othello) and She’s the Man (Twelfth Night) — plus Romeo + Juliet, which does the opposite and KEEPS the verse while moving everything else to Verona Beach. Omkara is the companion to Maqbool, Bhardwaj doing Othello in rural Uttar Pradesh. ⭐ Third, the films about performing Shakespeare rather than adaptations of him: Theatre of Blood, Rosencrantz & Guildenstern Are Dead, Prospero’s Books, Shakespeare-Wallah, Shakespeare in Love, and Caesar Must Die, in which prisoners in Rebibbia rehearse Julius Caesar and the rehearsal is the film. ⚠️ Lady Macbeth (2016) is NOT here and must not be added: it is Leskov’s Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District, a Russian novella that borrows only the epithet.

Session 12

The Victorian Novel

Eliot, Thackeray and Hardy give provincial life, social climbing and the land — and Trollope, newly held, gives the money. ⭐ The Way We Live Now was serialised in 1874-75 and its engine has not dated: Augustus Melmotte arrives in London from nowhere and floats a railway that is to run from Salt Lake City to Vera Cruz and is never going to be built. Men who know perfectly well it is vapour buy the shares anyway, because the price is rising. He is returned to Parliament for Westminster while the scheme is still inflating, and what finally destroys him is a forged signature rather than the fraud itself. He takes prussic acid. ⚠️ Running beside it is the antisemitism, and the series does not soften it: Brehgert, the Jewish banker whom Georgiana Longestaffe will not marry, is named thirty-eight times across the four episodes, and he conducts himself better over money than anyone in the book who objects to him. ⓘ Note what the shelf did NOT have until now — the fourth great Victorian novelist, and the one whose subject is finance. ⓘ Four of these eight are Hardy (Far from the Madding Crowd, Jude, Tess, The Mayor of Casterbridge), which is enough that a `hardy` corpus tag is owed.

Session 15

The Bloomsbury Group

Gordon Square, in three parts: the group as SUBJECT, Woolf as SOURCE, and her name doing work she had nothing to do with. First the circle itself — Carrington, Vita & Virginia, Tom & Viv and Wittgenstein credit Strachey, Dora Carrington, both Bells, Duncan Grant, Keynes, Ottoline Morrell, both Woolfs and Vita Sackville-West between them, and Wittgenstein is here because Keynes and Morrell are the ones who got him to Cambridge. ⭐ When this session was first written it carried an absence: the library held the group four times over as characters and NOT ONE adaptation of Virginia Woolf. That gap closed the same afternoon — Sally Potter’s Orlando and the 2026 Night & Day both arrived, so she is now source as well as subject, and Mrs Dalloway, To the Lighthouse and The Hours are the remaining holes. ⭐ Then the third thing, and the one worth the airtime, because it says what her name had come to MEAN by 1962. Albee: ‘I was in there having a beer one night, and I saw “Who’s afraid of Virginia Woolf?” scrawled in soap, I suppose, on this mirror… And it did strike me as being a rather typical, UNIVERSITY INTELLECTUAL JOKE.’ That is the whole point: by the fifties her name had stopped being a novelist and become a shorthand for highbrow academic culture — which is exactly the world the play is set in. George is in the history department (the phrase is said fifteen times), he is an associate professor at a New England college, and the library’s own tags for the film are `academia`, `campus`, `college professor`, `new england`. The joke only works on people who would be embarrassed not to have read her. ⚠️ And the film never explains it: search the subtitle and there is no Bloomsbury, no Mrs Dalloway, no To the Lighthouse, no Orlando, not even the word novelist — and no ‘big bad wolf’ either, because the Disney rights were too expensive, so Martha sings the line to ‘Here We Go Round the Mulberry Bush’ instead. ⓘ Leonard Woolf gave permission for the title. ⓘ The phrase was already in print: a 1957 New Yorker squib reports it as graffiti in a Greenwich Village espresso bar, five years before the play opened. ⚠️ None of this week’s tags go on it: it is not a Woolf adaptation and must never be filed as one. ⓘ And the one Bloomsbury novelist the shelf really does adapt is E. M. Forster, whose three films sit back in session 14, filed by their SETTING rather than by the circle he belonged to.

Session 17

The Inklings

Bloomsbury’s opposite number, and very nearly its mirror image: Oxford rather than Gordon Square, a pub rather than a drawing room, Christian and backward-looking where Bloomsbury was freethinking and modern. Here the library has the opposite problem too — it holds the Inklings’ WORK, which is the next session, and almost none of their life. Freud’s Last Session is the film that names them: Lewis in Hampstead on the eve of the war, arguing with Freud about God, Tolkien in the flashbacks, and the question asked outright in the dialogue — “what exactly are the Inklings?” ⛔ Tolkien (2019) is the trap, and worth naming in class: it is NOT an Inklings film. Its fellowship is the TCBS, four schoolboys from King Edward’s in Birmingham, and it ends on the Somme in 1916. The Inklings met at Oxford from the early 1930s. Same man, different circle, fifteen years apart. ⭐ Shadowlands is Lewis’s own half of the week and the one with the real Inkling in it: Edward Hardwicke plays his brother Warren — ‘Warnie’ — who was a member. ⚠️ It never says the word, though. The subtitle has no ‘Inklings’ anywhere and no Tolkien in the cast; the Oxford dons around Lewis are the play’s inventions. So of the three films here only one actually names the circle, which is a fair measure of how little of this life got filmed. ⭐ And one casting fact worth saying out loud in class: Anthony Hopkins plays Lewis here and Freud in Freud’s Last Session — the two sides of the same argument about God, same actor, thirty years apart.

Session 18

Tolkien and Lewis: the Invented Worlds

What the two of them actually made, set side by side. A philologist’s England reconstructed as myth and filmed in New Zealand, and a don’s Christian allegory that opens with children evacuated from the Blitz — both invented in the same Oxford pub conversation, and neither filmable until digital effects caught up, which is why all six of these are from 2001 or later. ⭐ And the tags, which are crowd-sourced TMDB keywords and so record what viewers actually see, draw the line between the two men on their own. All three Narnia films carry `christian allegory`, `christ allegory` AND `christ figure`. Of the three Lord of the Rings films, ONE carries `christian allegory` and none carries `christ figure`; Return of the King does not even carry `allegory`. Tolkien said he disliked allegory and preferred what he called applicability, Lewis wrote one on purpose, and three decades of strangers tagging films on the internet have reproduced that distinction without being asked to.

Session 19

Lake Geneva, 1816

One wet fortnight at the Villa Diodati, and the two genres that came out of it. Byron sets a ghost-story competition; Mary Godwin, eighteen, begins Frankenstein, and Polidori writes The Vampyre. Modern science fiction and the modern vampire were invented in the same room, in the summer that had no sun. Byron (2003) opens the session on the man rather than the fortnight: it runs from Caroline Lamb to Missolonghi and brings on Claire Clairmont, whose pursuit of Byron is what put the party at Geneva — but the film cuts straight from London to Venice and never stages the Diodati night at all.

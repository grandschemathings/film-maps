England by Its Invaders — a film syllabus

A second cut of the England shelf, taught as an argument rather than a timeline. The chronological course runs Roman Britain to the miners’ strike; this one asks a single question — who is arriving? — and follows it through six waves. ⭐ The terms ARE the waves, so the shape of the argument is the shape of the navigation. The films sit on seven different shelves: Rome’s Britain is filed under Ancient/Rome because those films are told from the invader’s end, the Hanoverian succession under MittelEuropa and Scotland because nobody filmed 1714 from London (the one London view is a composer’s biopic), and the climax of the French claim under Literature/Shakespeare because England remembers it as a play.

II · The Angles and the Saxons weeks 3–4 · 6 films

Week 3

The Invasion With No Film

⚠️ This session is an absence and should be taught as one. Nothing in the library depicts the adventus Saxonum — no Hengist, no Vortigern, no settlement. What we have is the Britons losing, told as myth: the Arthur cycle is the defeated side’s story, which is why it survives as legend rather than history. It is also the layer where the frame is most contestable, since migration-versus-conquest is live argument among historians. The week has to argue for its own existence.

Week 4

What They Left in the Ground

With no invasion film, the Saxons reach us as grave goods and a poem. ⭐ The Dig is the one great Anglo-Saxon film on the shelf and it is filed under Wars/WW2, because its frame is the summer of 1939 — Sutton Hoo excavated as the next war starts. Two of this course’s layers have their key film captured by the Second World War; the other is Olivier’s Henry V.

Week 9

A Century On, Still an Occupation

⭐ The core of the argument. Anouilh’s Becket is set in 1170 and plays England as an occupied country with a collaborator class — 29 uses of “Saxon” and 5 of “collaborat-” in the film’s own subtitle: “A Saxon knife — for a Saxon collaborator.” ⚠️ And it is wrong: the real Becket was Norman, from a Rouen merchant family. Anouilh is said to have found that out after writing it and kept it anyway. Factually wrong about the man, structurally right about the country — and written by someone who had staged Antigone in occupied Paris in 1944.

Week 10

Robert of Artois Sells England a Claim

The hinge, and the French tell it. The Capetian line fails in 1328; Salic law bars Isabella; and a disinherited French prince flees to London and talks Edward III into claiming the crown — because the English royal house is a French royal house. ⭐ Both held adaptations end on Le Lis et le Lion, the lilies quartered onto the English arms, and the 1972 series states the thesis in its closing narration: “Robert of Artois’ war wasn’t yet England’s war … He had fired up, between France and England, a war which was about to last 100 years.”

Week 11

The Deposition

Three depictions held, on three shelves, and the chronological course cites one. Jarman films Marlowe; Follett opens World Without End on Isabella imprisoning her husband and crowning their son; the French series tells the same deposition as La Louve de France. ⚠️ All three are fiction — there is no documentary here, and the murder at Berkeley is chronicle, not record. The red-hot poker is a story.

Week 12

Agincourt, and the Claim at Its Height

1415 and the Treaty of Troyes, which made an English king heir of France. ⭐ Filed under Literature/Shakespeare, correctly: we know this through a play written around 1599 for an English audience, and Olivier’s 1944 version is a D-Day morale picture. ⭐ The language flips on screen — where Becket has Normans sneering at Saxons, III.iv has a French princess conjugating English nouns, placed immediately after Harfleur falls. She starts learning the conqueror’s language the moment the English take a French town: “Ainsi dis-je: d’elbow, de nick, et de chin.” Katherine of Valois is Henry VII’s grandmother through Owen Tudor, so that scene produces the Tudors.

VI · The Germans weeks 16–18 · 10 films

Week 16

The Succession, From Both Ends

1714, and the last wave. ⭐ Saraband for Dead Lovers is the only holding that spans the hinge — a 1948 Ealing tragedy about Sophia Dorothea of Celle, imprisoned at Ahlden for thirty-two years by the man who became George I. It is filed on MittelEuropa, in a German court, because that is where the story is: the dynasty is still foreign at the moment it arrives. (Christopher Lee is in it, in his first year on screen, as Herzog Anthony von Wolfenbüttel.) ⭐ The Great Mr Handel (1942) is the other end, and the only London-side view of the dynasty in the library — filed under Biopic/Musician, because it is remembered as a composer’s story, not a king’s. It opens in 1727 on the succession itself: “He brings word that the king has died on the way to Hanover” → “Unless the Stuart faction takes its chance, Walpole will be well advised to hasten the coronation.” Then a bishop objects that George II’s four coronation anthems have gone to a German from the playhouse — “But surely some of our native composers might have been written here?” — and Handel answers “I am English by act of parliament … English by choice.” ⚠️ The dynasty did not bring its composer: Handel was the Elector’s Kapellmeister, in London on leave from 1710, and overstayed; in 1714 his employer followed him. So the pair runs Sophia Dorothea locked away at Ahlden by George I, then his son crowned in Westminster Abbey to Hanover’s music, with the Stuarts still waiting — which is session 17.

Week 18

We’re Not German Any More

The frame does not end with an expulsion — it ends with the invaders deleting their own name. In the summer of 1917, at war with Germany, the royal house drops Saxe-Coburg-Gotha for Windsor and its relatives anglicise Battenberg into Mountbatten. (The two halves are only spliced into Mountbatten-Windsor by an Order in Council in 1960.) ⭐ The session film is The Lost Prince (2003), which reached the shelf on 2026-10-14 and carries the argument in sequence. Poliakoff gives Part 2 ten minutes that run: “Your cousin, the Tsar, has just abdicated” → “because you have a German name!” → “Saxe… Coburg… Gotha” → Stamfordham proposing that “Edward III was often known as Edward of Windsor” → “Papa has rechristened the family the House of Windsor” → and then the reversal, “We agreed the Tsar and his family could come to Britain?” The renaming and the refusal of asylum are one scene, which is the session’s whole claim. ⚠️ It is told from inside the nursery of a disabled child the family hid, so the week should resist reading it only as constitutional history. Until it arrived, one line in the entire library said this, and not on the England shelf. The King’s Man (2021), filed under Wars/WW1, gives it to George V himself — and look at what he says it after: “The kaiser’s blockade is working. The people are hungry. The next revolution might be here. I’ve even been advised to change my German surname Saxe-Coburg to Windsor. More English.” The renaming arrives as an answer to revolution, moments after Russia leaves the war — which is historically exact, since the same king refused the Romanovs asylum that same summer. ⚠️ The King’s Speech contains no reference at all; Victoria and The Young Victoria say “Saxe-Coburg” only as Albert’s title, the house before it had anything to hide. ⚠️ And Battenberg → Mountbatten appears nowhere: every Mountbatten held is the man in India in 1947. The library has the consequence and never the act. ⭐ So the last wave is the only one that wins by ceasing to be foreign, out of fear, while its cousins’ dynasties fall — and that, not 1066, is where a course about invaders runs out.

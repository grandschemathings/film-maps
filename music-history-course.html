History through Music — a film syllabus

A history of Europe and America told through the people who made its music, and — more to the point — through whoever was paying them. The sequence is the argument: the church owns the voice, then the court hires the composer, then the composer quits and sells tickets, then the nation claims him, then a machine learns to store sound and the whole economy moves to America. ⭐ The hinge is session 14. Before the phonograph the thing sold is a PERFORMANCE and the musician is paid by a patron; after it the thing sold is a COPY and the musician is paid by a label. Everything from ragtime to hip-hop is a footnote to that change of noun. ⚠️ The second half of this course is built to show its own bias. Measured 2026-10-15 against the whole library: the British Invasion is held ten times over and Motown — the American Black music the British Invasion was covering — is held not once. Session 21 is therefore nearly empty, and session 25 ends the course in Detroit with a film about Eminem and no film about Berry Gordy. That is not a gap to apologise for; it is the finding, and it is what a library assembled from the Anglophone side of the Atlantic looks like when you ask it to account for itself.

Week 1

The Voice the Church Owned

Music before anybody bought it. Hildegard composes inside an institution that regards her output as devotion rather than property, and the film is unusual in taking the convent seriously as a WORKPLACE — an abbess negotiating with bishops over land and staff. ⛔ One film carries this session and that is the whole early-music holding. No Palestrina, no Monteverdi, no troubadour film, nothing on plainchant or the Notre-Dame school. Eight hundred years of European music have a single title.

Week 3

The Voice They Manufactured

⭐ The castrato is the clearest case in the course of a body altered to meet a market. Thousands of boys were cut in 18th-century Italy; a handful became the highest-paid performers in Europe and the rest disappeared. Farinelli sang the same few arias to Philip V of Spain nightly for a decade as a medical treatment. The film is lurid and the synthesised voice is a fake, but the economics are exact.

Week 4

The Exception: Opera Already Has a Box Office

⭐ Opera is the hole in this term's thesis, and the course is sharper for admitting it. Term I says music before anybody bought it — but a paying public had been buying opera tickets in Venice since 1637, when the Teatro San Cassiano opened as the first commercial opera house in Europe. By 1764 Venice is a crowded market, and Il Boemo is about a Czech nobody trying to break into it: Josef Mysliveček, living precariously, chasing a commission, and the film is unusually interested in the impresarios, the fees and who has to be flattered to get a libretto. That is a music business, a century before Mozart walks out on the Archbishop in session 6. ⭐ The library holds five complete operas filmed as operas, and three of them are here. Losey's Don Giovanni is staged in Palladio's buildings around Vicenza, the Teatro Olimpico and the Villa Rotonda, with barges moving through the Venetian marshes. It relocates a Spanish legend to the Veneto and lets the architecture carry the argument. ⭐ Bergman's The Magic Flute is the session's thesis in one work. Mozart did not write it for a court. It premiered in September 1791 at the Theater auf der Wieden, Emanuel Schikaneder's commercial theatre in the Vienna suburbs, with Schikaneder as librettist, impresario and Papageno: a popular Singspiel for a paying public, ten weeks before Mozart died. Bergman films it in Swedish inside a reconstruction of the Drottningholm court theatre, and turns the camera on the audience during the overture, so the film is about who is in the seats as much as what is on the stage. The Tales of Hoffmann is the other pole: Powell and Pressburger take Offenbach's last opera, sung in English throughout, and make it as pure cinema, where Losey made it architecture. The other two filmed operas are La traviata in session 11 and The Mikado in session 13. ⚠️ Nothing here from before 1700. No Monteverdi, no Orfeo, nothing on the Florentine Camerata. The form arrives in this library already a hundred and fifty years old and already commercial.

Week 5

Bach: Music as a Job

⭐ Three films and a series on one composer, and they agree on the unglamorous reading. Straub and Huillet shoot Bach as a municipal employee filing cantatas weekly, narrated by his wife over account books and contracts of employment, with the music played straight through in long takes and no drama whatever. It is the most radical film in this course precisely because it refuses the genius story the next two sessions depend on.

Week 6

Mozart Quits

In 1781 Mozart is literally kicked down the stairs out of the Archbishop of Salzburg's service and becomes a freelance in Vienna. Everything after is the experiment of whether a composer can live on concerts, pupils and publishers instead of a post. ⭐ Beaumarchais is the session's hidden spine and the library had him filed as a musician without anyone saying why. He wrote The Barber of Seville and The Marriage of Figaro; Louis XVI read the second one and banned it outright, Beaumarchais got it staged anyway in 1784, and Mozart set the banned play to music two years later. Napoleon called it the Revolution already in action. The same man was a watchmaker, a spy, and the arms dealer who shipped the muskets that won Saratoga — so the comic opera, the French Revolution and the American one all run through one biography. ⭐ Against all of it, Chevalier — Joseph Bologne, fencing champion, violinist, born enslaved in Guadeloupe, denied the Paris Opéra because three divas refused to be directed by a Black man. Same decade, same city, same market, and the market is not open to everyone.

Week 7

Beethoven and the Invention of the Genius

⭐ Four Beethoven films, which is more than the library holds on any other composer, and the hinge of the course. Beethoven takes subscriptions from aristocrats but will not wear their livery; he tears up the Eroica dedication when Napoleon crowns himself. The deafness matters here as economics as well as tragedy — a composer who cannot perform must be paid for the SCORE. Pair with Kubrick for what the next century did with the Ninth: the Ode to Joy as the soundtrack to state violence.

Week 8

The Virtuoso and the Tour

⭐ Liszt is the first rock star and Ken Russell is the only director who ever said so out loud — Roger Daltrey plays him, women tear his clothes off, and the word Lisztomania was coined by Heine in 1844 to describe an actual medical panic about audience hysteria. Paganini was rumoured to have sold his soul and did nothing to deny it. What the railway and the piano manufacturer built together was the touring celebrity.

Week 9

The National Schools — Chopin's Poland, Grieg's Norway

⭐ The era Alfred's audiobook history grouped together, and the right call: from about 1850 the composer stops being European and starts being Norwegian, Czech, Finnish, Russian. It is the 1848 generation set to music — collect the folk tunes, build a concert repertoire out of them, and you have manufactured a national tradition that can be claimed to be ancient. Chopin writes mazurkas in Paris exile after the November Uprising; Grieg does for Norway what Ibsen was doing in the theatre next door. ⚠️ Two nations out of ten. See the WANTED list — Smetana, Dvořák, Sibelius, Bartók, Falla and Elgar are all unheld, and this is the largest single hole in the course. Mussorgsky has arrived, in session 12.

Week 11

Opera as the National Theatre

Once the nation claims the composer it claims the opera house too, and the building becomes the room where a country shows itself what it is. Zeffirelli's La traviata is the second complete filmed opera here — Verdi in 1853, whose name Risorgimento crowds chanted as an acrostic for Vittorio Emanuele Re D'Italia, so that cheering the composer was cheering the king and the police could do nothing about it. Saura takes Bizet's French fantasy of Spain and hands it back to Spanish dancers, who argue with it for ninety minutes. ⭐ The Barber of Siberia is the best case in the session and it was sitting on the Russia shelf, never read as music. Imperial cadets in the 1880s rehearse and perform The Marriage of Figaro as a school exercise inside a military academy — the same Beaumarchais play Louis XVI banned in session 6. A century after a king tried to suppress it, the Tsar's officer corps stages it as an accomplishment. ⭐ And Fitzcarraldo takes the idea to its absurd end: a man drags a steamship over a mountain to build an opera house in the Amazon and bring Caruso to it, because that is what he believes civilisation is. ⚠️ One film here is not an opera at all. Pasolini's Medea is the library's only Maria Callas, and he cast the most famous voice of the century in a part where she never sings.

Week 12

Russia, and the Composer as State Property

Tchaikovsky is kept by a patron he is contractually forbidden to meet, and Russell films the marriage as a catastrophe. ⭐ The Five arrive in one film, and the film is itself an exhibit. Roshal's Mussorgsky follows the circle at Balakirev's from 1858, with Borodin, Rimsky-Korsakov and the critic Stasov, through the Mariinsky's refusal of Boris Godunov to its staging in 1874. ⚠️ Watch it for 1950 as much as for 1874: it was made two years after the Party's 1948 decree on music denounced Shostakovich and Prokofiev for formalism, and it gives the Five exactly the virtues the decree demanded — folk song, the people, realism, Russia against the West. So the session's subject, the composer as state property, is on screen twice: once in the story, and once in the film. The Concert reaches the other end of the century: a Bolshoi orchestra purged by Brezhnev for its Jewish players, reassembling thirty years later.

Week 13

Pirates: the First Copyright War

⭐ Before anyone could own a recording, the fight was already about who owned the performance — and this session is the rehearsal for session 15. H.M.S. Pinafore was pirated across America by more than a hundred and fifty companies that rewrote the text and paid Gilbert, Sullivan and D'Oyly Carte nothing, because a published score let any impresario mount the work with his own orchestration. The answer was The Pirates of Penzance, premiered in New York on 31 December 1879 to secure American copyright — with a scratch performance thrown on the day before at the Royal Bijou Theatre in Paignton, Devon, to hold the British rights. One opera, two countries, twenty-four hours apart, and both performances staged to satisfy lawyers rather than audiences. The title is not a coincidence. Mike Leigh's Topsy-Turvy is the same partnership five years later, grinding out The Mikado under contract and visibly bored with each other — and then the opera itself, which the library has had all along and had never been asked for. ⚠️ It is shelved on Japan. An English comic opera about an invented Japan that Gilbert admitted was a satire of England, filed by its setting, carrying exactly two tags — musical and japan — and therefore invisible to every search for opera this course ran. Watch it straight after Topsy-Turvy, which is two and a half hours on the making of it. ⭐ And the Marx Brothers close the session in a film whose most quoted scene is two men tearing a singer's contract apart clause by clause until nothing is left but the sanity clause. ⭐ Two films then carry the fight past the score and into the record, which is session 14 and 15 arriving early. The Great Caruso is the singer Fitzcarraldo drags a ship over a mountain to hear in session 11, and the first opera star the phonograph made rather than the house. Diva (1981) runs the argument backwards: a soprano who refuses on principle ever to record, and a Paris postman who bootlegs her from the stalls on a tape recorder, so that the only copy of the voice is a pirate one.

⭐ The Mikado is downstream of a gunboat, and the library holds both ends of the chain. In 1853 Commodore Matthew Perry sailed four black ships into Edo Bay and ended two centuries of sakoku at cannon-point. Within a decade Japanese goods were in Paris shops and Japanese woodblock prints — ukiyo-e, often arriving as packing material — were being collected by European painters; the critic Philippe Burty named the craze japonisme in 1872. By the 1880s it had reached the London stage, and this session is sitting in the middle of it without saying so. ⭐ Topsy-Turvy is a film about japonisme and The Mikado is the product of it. Leigh stages the origin legend directly: Gilbert is taken to the Japanese Village exhibition in Knightsbridge, buys a samurai sword, and the sword falls off his study wall and gives him the opera. ⚠️ The dates do not support the legend — the Village opened in January 1885 and Gilbert had sent Sullivan the plot in May 1884 — and Leigh keeps it anyway, which tells you how completely japonisme was accepted as the explanation for anything Japanese-looking. The film also shows the other half: Japanese women brought in from the exhibition to teach English singers how to move. ⭐ And watch the two films in this session back to back, because The Mikado tells the same story first and gets the date right. The 1983 film opens on a narrator saying konnichiwa and explaining, before a note is sung, that there was a great Japanese exhibition in Knightsbridge in 1885, that Gilbert lived nearby in South Kensington and went to look, and that seeing the people behind the figures on the vases and fans gave him the setting — then goes straight on to the jealousy between a knighted Sir Arthur Sullivan and a still-plain Mr Gilbert, which is the entire subject of Topsy-Turvy sixteen years later. The cheap 1983 television version is the better history. ⭐ The French painter is Monet, and he is held. More than two hundred Japanese prints hung in the house at Giverny — Hiroshige, Hokusai, Utamaro — and he built the Japanese footbridge into the water garden and then painted it for thirty years, which is japonisme carried from a print into a garden and back onto a canvas. ⚠️ But I, Claude Monet (2017), the one film held on him, never mentions any of it — the only Japanese thing in its whole script is a peony. The painter carries the connection; the film does not. ⭐ Two films in the library do carry it, in dialogue, and neither is about Monet. In Vincent & Theo the brothers have a row about money and Theo shouts that they have a hundred Japanese prints already — Vincent has spent the paint money on more. In Lust for Life, Gauguin holds one up and says the Japanese paint as simply as we breathe. Van Gogh is the hard case and the library has him five times over: he did not adapt ukiyo-e, he copied Hiroshige outright. ⭐ And the far end of the chain is on the Japan shelf, tagged and never connected to any of this. Assassination (1964) opens with the four black ships in Edo Bay in 1853 and follows the collapse of the Tokugawa shogunate; Samurai Marathon (2019) is a daimyo in 1855 making his samurai run because Perry has shown him what is coming. Both carry the tags Commodore Perry and black ships. Silk (2007) is the commerce: a Frenchman crossing to Japan in the 1860s for silkworm eggs, along the same trade route that was carrying the prints to Paris. ⚠️ Watch them as the first act of this session and The Mikado stops being a curiosity and becomes a consequence.

Week 14

1927: Sound Arrives Wearing Burnt Cork

⭐ The hinge of the whole syllabus, and it is an ugly one. The first feature with synchronised sound is about a cantor's son who makes it on Broadway in blackface. Everything this course has said about patronage ends here: from now on the thing sold is a copy, not an evening. Ragtime and Joplin set up what was being copied FROM — Joplin sold sheet music, died broke, and spent his last years failing to get his opera staged.

Week 15

Who Owns the Master

⭐ Ma Rainey's Black Bottom is the single best film in the library about the economics of recorded music — the entire play happens in and around a Chicago studio in 1927 while a white producer waits for a Black woman to sign, and she knows exactly what her voice is worth to him and makes him wait. Sinners takes the same decade into the Delta. Read beside the Cotton Club, where the performers are Black and the audience is not admitted if they are. ⭐ Against all of it, O Brother, Where Art Thou? — the identical transaction played as farce. Three escaped convicts walk into a Mississippi radio station in 1937, cut Man of Constant Sorrow for ten dollars a man from a blind proprietor, and spend the rest of the picture being chased while the record becomes a regional hit they know nothing about and earn nothing from. A decade apart, the same flat fee, and opposite ends of knowing: Ma Rainey understands exactly what she is signing away and makes the producer wait for it; the Soggy Bottom Boys have no idea. ⭐ And one film here sits on the other side of the desk. Herb Alpert Is... is the only portrait in this course of a musician who ended up owning the masters — Tijuana Brass trumpeter, then co-founder of A&M Records with Jerry Moss in 1962, which grew into one of the largest independent labels in the world. Everybody else in this session signs something. Alpert is the one who got to be the company.

⭐ A&M is the thread that runs out of this session into the last two, and nothing in the library says so. Alpert and Jerry Moss signed the Sex Pistols on 10 March 1977 and dropped them about six days later, after the party at the London office. The famous photograph outside Buckingham Palace is a Malcolm McLaren stunt — the contract had been signed the day before — so even the signing was a product. Week 17

The Collector and the Plug

⭐ This session has a perfect pair of bookends and they are fifty years apart. Songcatcher is a musicologist in 1907 Appalachia writing down ballads on a wax cylinder because she believes they are dying; the Newport footage is Dylan in 1965 plugging in and the folk establishment — the people who collected those ballads — booing him for it. Authenticity is invented by the collectors and then enforced against the musicians. ⭐ And then it pays off sixty years late: the O Brother, Where Art Thou? soundtrack put pre-war repertoire in front of a mass audience with almost no country-radio play, took Album of the Year at the 2002 Grammys, and won Ralph Stanley one for O Death sung a cappella at seventy-four. Lily Penleric spends her film trying to get this music heard; a Coen brothers comedy does it.

Week 21

What They Were Covering — the session the library cannot teach

⛔ This is the hole, and it is the biggest one in the collection. The British Invasion is held eight ways. Motown is held zero — no film about Berry Gordy, the Funk Brothers, Hitsville, the Supremes or the Temptations, and nothing on the Alabama studio where the Stones cut Brown Sugar and Wild Horses in December 1969. ⭐ But the session turns out to have one real film, and it is a 1980 comedy. The Blues Brothers puts Cab Calloway, James Brown, Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin and John Lee Hooker on screen performing as themselves — and its backing band includes Steve Cropper and Donald 'Duck' Dunn, the Stax house rhythm section, the men who actually played on the Otis Redding and Sam & Dave records this session is about. So Stax is in the library after all, uncredited as such, inside a film nobody files under music history. ⭐ It is also the argument in miniature: two white men in suits doing the covering one generation after the Beatles did, except that this time they brought the originators with them and put them on the poster. Calloway is the thread to follow — played by an actor in session 15, here he is himself, in his seventies, singing Minnie the Moocher. ⭐ And the session has one more film that owes nothing to any white intermediary at all: Soul Power, the three-day Kinshasa festival of 1974 — James Brown, B.B. King, Bill Withers, Celia Cruz — cut from the same Leon Gast shoot that produced When We Were Kings, which is filed on Sports and has never been read as a music film. American soul music carried back to Africa by the people who made it. Beside all of it, Aretha at Atlantic (whose records the Muscle Shoals players are ON), Sam Cooke in a Miami motel room arguing about exactly this, and Ray Charles inventing most of it. ⚠️ Everything in the WANTED list below is still a first-rank acquisition target.

Week 23

Punk: Two Cities, Both Half-Missing

⚠️ Two films, and between them they miss the thing. CBGB is the New York side — Patti Smith, Blondie, Ramones, a Bowery bar — and Sid and Nancy is an English film about the London side DYING in a New York hotel. Week 24

Seattle — exactly two films, and they are the right two

⭐ The one scene the library documents properly, and it does it in a fiction film and a documentary made four years apart. Singles is shot in 1991 while it is happening, with members of Pearl Jam and Soundgarden standing around in the background of a romantic comedy, released before anyone knew. Hype! is 1996 and is explicitly about the feeding frenzy — the A&R men arriving, the Sub Pop joke about hyping a scene into existence that stopped being a joke. Watch them in order and you have the whole life cycle of a scene in four years.

Week 25

Hip-Hop, and the Course Ends in Detroit

⭐ Session 21 asked why there is no Motown film. This session answers it badly and the badness is the point. The library holds one film about making hip-hop — 8 Mile — and it is set in Detroit, thirty years after Berry Gordy left it, and it is about a white rapper. There is no Wild Style, no Style Wars, no Krush Groove, no Straight Outta Compton, no Notorious. The other films here use hip-hop as atmosphere rather than subject: Radio Raheem's boombox, the banlieue in La Haine, RZA scoring a samurai picture. ⚠️ Four decades of the most consequential popular music in the world, and one film about anybody making it.

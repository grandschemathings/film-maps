History through Music — a film syllabus

A history of Europe and America told through the people who made its music, and — more to the point — through whoever was paying them. The sequence is the argument: the church owns the voice, then the court hires the composer, then the composer quits and sells tickets, then the nation claims him, then a machine learns to store sound and the whole economy moves to America. ⭐ The hinge is session 11. Before the phonograph the thing sold is a PERFORMANCE and the musician is paid by a patron; after it the thing sold is a COPY and the musician is paid by a label. Everything from ragtime to hip-hop is a footnote to that change of noun. ⚠️ The second half of this course is built to show its own bias. Measured 2026-10-15 against the whole library: the British Invasion is held ten times over and Motown — the American Black music the British Invasion was covering — is held not once. Session 18 is therefore nearly empty, and session 22 ends the course in Detroit with a film about Eminem and no film about Berry Gordy. That is not a gap to apologise for; it is the finding, and it is what a library assembled from the Anglophone side of the Atlantic looks like when you ask it to account for itself.

I · Before the Market weeks 1–3 · 4 films

Week 1

The Voice the Church Owned

Music before anybody bought it. Hildegard composes inside an institution that regards her output as devotion rather than property, and the film is unusual in taking the convent seriously as a WORKPLACE — an abbess negotiating with bishops over land and staff. ⛔ One film carries this session and that is the whole early-music holding. No Palestrina, no Monteverdi, no troubadour film, nothing on plainchant or the Notre-Dame school. Eight hundred years of European music have a single title.

Week 2

The Court Musician

The next paymaster is a king. Sainte-Colombe refuses Louis XIV and teaches Marais, who takes the job — the film is two hours on whether you can serve a court and still mean it, played on viols in near-darkness. Beside it, Molière and Lully at work on the comédie-ballet, which is the court buying entertainment by the evening.

Week 3

The Voice They Manufactured

⭐ The castrato is the clearest case in the course of a body altered to meet a market. Thousands of boys were cut in 18th-century Italy; a handful became the highest-paid performers in Europe and the rest disappeared. Farinelli sang the same few arias to Philip V of Spain nightly for a decade as a medical treatment. The film is lurid and the synthesised voice is a fake, but the economics are exact.

II · Employed, Then Free weeks 4–6 · 13 films

Week 4

Bach: Music as a Job

⭐ Three films and a series on one composer, and they agree on the unglamorous reading. Straub and Huillet shoot Bach as a municipal employee filing cantatas weekly, narrated by his wife over account books and contracts of employment, with the music played straight through in long takes and no drama whatever. It is the most radical film in this course precisely because it refuses the genius story the next two sessions depend on.

Week 5

Mozart Quits

In 1781 Mozart is literally kicked down the stairs out of the Archbishop of Salzburg's service and becomes a freelance in Vienna. Everything after is the experiment of whether a composer can live on concerts, pupils and publishers instead of a post. ⭐ Beaumarchais is the session's hidden spine and the library had him filed as a musician without anyone saying why. He wrote The Barber of Seville and The Marriage of Figaro; Louis XVI read the second one and banned it outright, Beaumarchais got it staged anyway in 1784, and Mozart set the banned play to music two years later. Napoleon called it the Revolution already in action. The same man was a watchmaker, a spy, and the arms dealer who shipped the muskets that won Saratoga — so the comic opera, the French Revolution and the American one all run through one biography. ⭐ Against all of it, Chevalier — Joseph Bologne, fencing champion, violinist, born enslaved in Guadeloupe, denied the Paris Opéra because three divas refused to be directed by a Black man. Same decade, same city, same market, and the market is not open to everyone.

Week 6

Beethoven and the Invention of the Genius

⭐ Four Beethoven films, which is more than the library holds on any other composer, and the hinge of the course. Beethoven takes subscriptions from aristocrats but will not wear their livery; he tears up the Eroica dedication when Napoleon crowns himself. The deafness matters here as economics as well as tragedy — a composer who cannot perform must be paid for the SCORE. Pair with Kubrick for what the next century did with the Ninth: the Ode to Joy as the soundtrack to state violence.

III · The Nation Sings weeks 7–10 · 13 films

Week 7

The Virtuoso and the Tour

⭐ Liszt is the first rock star and Ken Russell is the only director who ever said so out loud — Roger Daltrey plays him, women tear his clothes off, and the word Lisztomania was coined by Heine in 1844 to describe an actual medical panic about audience hysteria. Paganini was rumoured to have sold his soul and did nothing to deny it. What the railway and the piano manufacturer built together was the touring celebrity.

Week 8

The National Schools — Chopin's Poland, Grieg's Norway

⭐ The era Alfred's audiobook history grouped together, and the right call: from about 1850 the composer stops being European and starts being Norwegian, Czech, Finnish, Russian. It is the 1848 generation set to music — collect the folk tunes, build a concert repertoire out of them, and you have manufactured a national tradition that can be claimed to be ancient. Chopin writes mazurkas in Paris exile after the November Uprising; Grieg does for Norway what Ibsen was doing in the theatre next door. ⚠️ Two nations out of ten. See the WANTED list — Smetana, Dvořák, Sibelius, Mussorgsky, Bartók, Falla and Elgar are all unheld, and this is the largest single hole in the course.

Week 9

Wagner and the German Idea

⭐ Held in ten parts and nobody has watched it as history. The 1983 Burton series is nine hours on the man who talked a king into building him a theatre, invented the darkened auditorium and the hidden orchestra, wrote the antisemitic pamphlet, and handed the next century a vocabulary it used for everything. Visconti's Ludwig is the same story from the paymaster's end — a king bankrupting Bavaria on an opera house and a castle.

Week 10

Russia, and the Composer as State Property

Tchaikovsky is kept by a patron he is contractually forbidden to meet, and Russell films the marriage as a catastrophe. ⚠️ The Five are missing entirely — no Mussorgsky, no Rimsky-Korsakov, no Borodin, so the actual nationalist argument in Russian music has no film. The Concert reaches the other end of the century: a Bolshoi orchestra purged by Brezhnev for its Jewish players, reassembling thirty years later.

Week 11

1927: Sound Arrives Wearing Burnt Cork

⭐ The hinge of the whole syllabus, and it is an ugly one. The first feature with synchronised sound is about a cantor's son who makes it on Broadway in blackface. Everything this course has said about patronage ends here: from now on the thing sold is a copy, not an evening. Ragtime and Joplin set up what was being copied FROM — Joplin sold sheet music, died broke, and spent his last years failing to get his opera staged.

Week 12

Who Owns the Master

⭐ Ma Rainey's Black Bottom is the single best film in the library about the economics of recorded music — the entire play happens in and around a Chicago studio in 1927 while a white producer waits for a Black woman to sign, and she knows exactly what her voice is worth to him and makes him wait. Sinners takes the same decade into the Delta. Read beside the Cotton Club, where the performers are Black and the audience is not admitted if they are. ⭐ Against all of it, O Brother, Where Art Thou? — the identical transaction played as farce. Three escaped convicts walk into a Mississippi radio station in 1937, cut Man of Constant Sorrow for ten dollars a man from a blind proprietor, and spend the rest of the picture being chased while the record becomes a regional hit they know nothing about and earn nothing from. A decade apart, the same flat fee, and opposite ends of knowing: Ma Rainey understands exactly what she is signing away and makes the producer wait for it; the Soggy Bottom Boys have no idea.

Swing Becomes an Industry

Week 14

The Collector and the Plug

⭐ This session has a perfect pair of bookends and they are fifty years apart. Songcatcher is a musicologist in 1907 Appalachia writing down ballads on a wax cylinder because she believes they are dying; the Newport footage is Dylan in 1965 plugging in and the folk establishment — the people who collected those ballads — booing him for it. Authenticity is invented by the collectors and then enforced against the musicians. ⭐ And then it pays off sixty years late: the O Brother, Where Art Thou? soundtrack put pre-war repertoire in front of a mass audience with almost no country-radio play, took Album of the Year at the 2002 Grammys, and won Ralph Stanley one for O Death sung a cappella at seventy-four. Lily Penleric spends her film trying to get this music heard; a Coen brothers comedy does it.

Week 18

What They Were Covering — the session the library cannot teach

⛔ This is the hole, and it is the biggest one in the collection. The British Invasion is held eight ways. Motown is held zero — no film about Berry Gordy, the Funk Brothers, Hitsville, the Supremes or the Temptations, and nothing on the Alabama studio where the Stones cut Brown Sugar and Wild Horses in December 1969. ⭐ But the session turns out to have one real film, and it is a 1980 comedy. The Blues Brothers puts Cab Calloway, James Brown, Ray Charles, Aretha Franklin and John Lee Hooker on screen performing as themselves — and its backing band includes Steve Cropper and Donald 'Duck' Dunn, the Stax house rhythm section, the men who actually played on the Otis Redding and Sam & Dave records this session is about. So Stax is in the library after all, uncredited as such, inside a film nobody files under music history. ⭐ It is also the argument in miniature: two white men in suits doing the covering one generation after the Beatles did, except that this time they brought the originators with them and put them on the poster. Calloway is the thread to follow — played by an actor in session 12, here he is himself, in his seventies, singing Minnie the Moocher. Beside it, Aretha at Atlantic (whose records the Muscle Shoals players are ON), Sam Cooke in a Miami motel room arguing about exactly this, and Ray Charles inventing most of it. ⚠️ Everything in the WANTED list below is still a first-rank acquisition target.

Week 19

The Last Waltz closes it in 1976 with a band quitting the road.

Week 20

Punk: Two Cities, Both Half-Missing

⚠️ Two films, and between them they miss the thing. CBGB is the New York side — Patti Smith, Blondie, Ramones, a Bowery bar — and Sid and Nancy is an English film about the London side DYING in a New York hotel. So the library holds punk's American birth and its American death and almost nothing of the London scene in between: no Filth and the Fury, no Rude Boy, no Great Rock 'n' Roll Swindle, no 24 Hour Party People, no Control. ⭐ Worth saying out loud that punk is the second British invasion and the library treats it as an American story.

Week 21

Seattle — exactly two films, and they are the right two

⭐ The one scene the library documents properly, and it does it in a fiction film and a documentary made four years apart. Singles is shot in 1991 while it is happening, with members of Pearl Jam and Soundgarden standing around in the background of a romantic comedy, released before anyone knew. Hype! is 1996 and is explicitly about the feeding frenzy — the A&R men arriving, the Sub Pop joke about hyping a scene into existence that stopped being a joke. Watch them in order and you have the whole life cycle of a scene in four years.

Week 22

Hip-Hop, and the Course Ends in Detroit

⭐ Session 18 asked why there is no Motown film. This session answers it badly and the badness is the point. The library holds one film about making hip-hop — 8 Mile — and it is set in Detroit, thirty years after Berry Gordy left it, and it is about a white rapper. There is no Wild Style, no Style Wars, no Krush Groove, no Straight Outta Compton, no Notorious. The other films here use hip-hop as atmosphere rather than subject: Radio Raheem's boombox, the banlieue in La Haine, RZA scoring a samurai picture. ⚠️ Four decades of the most consequential popular music in the world, and one film about anybody making it.

Week 23

Coda: Who Pays Now

Back to the concert hall, where the money never fully went away and the authority did.

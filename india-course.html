History of India — a film syllabus

A three-term chronological course in which the weekly work is a FILM rather than a book. The India shelf holds 56 items, but a national course cannot be taught from one shelf: Lagaan is filed under Sports, Maqbool and Omkara under Literature, Sajjan Singh Rangroot and The Forgotten Army under Wars, and Gangs of Wasseypur is not filed anywhere. The shape of the collection is itself the first lesson. Roughly half of what is held was made by outsiders looking in — Kipling adaptations, frontier adventures, Merchant Ivory, four Jungle Books — and the Indian-made films cluster heavily after 1980. The two centuries between the Mughals and the Mutiny are nearly empty, and the canon of Indian cinema is almost entirely absent: Mughal-e-Azam, Sholay and Mother India ALL arrived in October 2026, so the three canonical absences this course was written around are now closed and its opening thesis no longer holds — which is itself the lesson about what a collection is at any one moment. Satyajit Ray is here four times — the Apu Trilogy arrived in the same month, having sat unshelved on the same disk while this syllabus said he appeared once. Sessions are built from what is here and say plainly where the gaps are.

Week 3

The Ramayana, and Who Gets to Tell It

Two tellings of the epic, and neither is live action — ⭐ THE ONLY TWO RAMAYANA FILMS ON THIS SHELF ARE BOTH CARTOONS, which is Alfred’s observation and a real one: the oldest story in the course survives here only as animation — and the telling that actually mattered to Indians, Ramanand Sagar’s live-action serial, is the one not held. See the wanted entry below; the gap is the lesson. Nina Paley, an American, tells it through 1920s Annette Hanshaw recordings and frames it with her own divorce. Yugo Sako, Japanese, made his as a Japan–India co-production with Ram Mohan animating and Vanraj Bhatia scoring. ⭐ AND BOTH WERE CONTESTED BY THE SAME OBJECTION — who may tell it. The Vishwa Hindu Parishad protested to the Indian government over a foreigner handling the epic; Sako met their leaders, explained the project, and they were satisfied. What stopped the film was not the objection but the calendar: it premiered at the International Film Festival of India in January 1993, weeks after the Babri Masjid was demolished in December 1992, with riots in Bombay and Calcutta, and a Hindu-mythology film was judged too incendiary to release. It was banned three years, and only reached a wide Indian theatrical release in 2024 — thirty-one years after its premiere. Paley’s film met Hindu objections of its own, plus a copyright fight over the Hanshaw songs. ⚠️ The held copy is the FULL version (128.9 min at 25fps PAL = 135 min at 24), not the 97-minute US “Prince of Light” cut, and its only subtitle is YouTube auto-captions.

  • Sita Sings the Blues 2008 India/MoviesRVW
  • Ramayana : The Legend of Prince Rama 1993 MoviesRVW
  • Ramayan (Ramanand Sagar) 1987tvnot held⭐ THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT ABSENCE IN THIS SESSION, and arguably in the course. Doordarshan ran it weekly from 25 January 1987 to 31 July 1988 — 78 episodes, with Uttar Ramayan adding 39 more to March 1989, so 117 in all. Over eighty million people watched, streets emptied for the Sunday morning broadcast, and it is the reason a whole generation pictures Rama the way it does. Its politics are inseparable from its reach: it ran straight into the Ayodhya campaign, and the Babri Masjid was demolished in December 1992, three and a half years after the last episode. ⛔ Everything held in this session is animated and made by outsiders; the live-action telling made in India, for India, that became a national event is the thing missing. Week 18

Nehru’s India and the Bengal Screen

Ray’s Apu grows from a Bengali village to Calcutta across three films and fourteen years, while Ghatak films the refugees that city was absorbing and Merchant Ivory film the English leaving it. Three accounts of one decade — from inside, from underneath, and on the way out, with the 2024 documentary on Merchant Ivory itself as the fourth: the partnership looking back at what it was doing here. ⭐ A THREAD WORTH FOLLOWING OUT OF THIS SESSION. All three Apu films were scored by Ravi Shankar, who is credited as composer in each of their nfos. Shankar taught George Harrison the sitar, and the two of them appear together in session 19’s Concert for Bangladesh — so the music of a Bengali village in 1955 reaches a benefit stage at Madison Square Garden in 1971 by a line you can trace through this library’s own cast lists.

Mother India arrived in October 2026 — the LAST of the three canonical absences this course was built around, Mehboob Khan’s 1957 answer to Katherine Mayo and the film that defines what popular Hindi cinema could carry. ⚠️ The held copy runs 149.7 min at 25fps, about 156 min of film against the canonical 172, so it is the export cut and not the full one. Week 27

The Guru and the Western Imagination

What the West goes to India to find: Hesse’s invented Buddha, a Los Angeles yogi, a Dahl story about a man who learns to see without his eyes, and a piano carried into the Himalaya. ⭐ THE GERMAN STRAND runs through this session and is worth teaching as its own half. Hesse published Siddhartha in 1922 — interwar German Indophilia at its purest, written by a man who had reached Ceylon and Sumatra in 1911 but never India itself. Seventeen years later Heinrich Harrer left for Nanga Parbat, was interned by the British in India when war broke out, and walked to Lhasa; Wikidata records his membership of both the Sturmabteilung and the Schutzstaffel, which surfaced publicly in 1997 while Seven Years in Tibet was being made. Same fascination, two decades and a uniform apart. ⛔ BUT NOTE WHAT THE FILMS CAN ACTUALLY CARRY. Siddhartha (1972) is set in ancient India and its subtitle contains no mention of Germany, Europe or the twentieth century at all. It is the ARTEFACT of that fascination, not a depiction of it — the novel is the interwar document, the film is not. Seven Years in Tibet is the only held film set inside the encounter.

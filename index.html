Film Maps

Views onto a history film collection. The maps place each film by where it is set — not where it was made, and not where its studio was. The syllabi ask a different question: what could you actually teach from this shelf? Each course can carry an optional extension — a shorter module on the cinema itself. A crosswalk asks a third: where do two different tellings of the same history actually touch?

Work on Film

A claim from a film forum — that films were about workers until Norma Rae and are now about bankers — tested against the collection. There turn out to be four phases, not two, and the worker does not vanish in 1979: he changes desks, and then the camera changes floors. Three controlled pairs carry the argument; the collection holds half of one.

Two Tellings of the Tudors

Three BBC2 studio serials from 1970–72 set against three Philippa Gregory adaptations from 2008–20, matched part to part wherever the two tell the same year. Hover any episode and the opposite column rolls until its matches come level. One 90-minute play covers the years Gregory gives seventeen episodes.

History of Russia — a film syllabus

Thirty sessions across three terms, Kievan Rus to Navalny, in which the weekly work is a film rather than a book. A session with no film is a gap in the collection, not in the history — so the thin weeks are the point.

History of England — a film syllabus

Roman Britain to the miners' strike, across Michaelmas, Hilary and Trinity. The deepest shelf for television here — several weeks are carried by a serial rather than a feature.

England by Its Invaders — a film syllabus

The same shelf cut as an argument rather than a timeline: six waves of arrival, Rome to Hanover, where every chapter is a successful invasion and the last two were invited. The terms are the waves. Only 23 of its 55 films are on the England shelf — Roman Britain is filed under Ancient Rome, Agincourt under Shakespeare, and the German succession under MittelEuropa and Scotland.

18 sessions · 58 films placed · 6 terms

No Footage Exists — history’s unfilmed catastrophes

A library of history on film has a hole exactly where the twentieth century’s largest fears are, and the hole has a cause: the things most feared did not happen, so nobody filmed them. There is no documentary of a continent destroyed by nuclear war. The only record of how 1983 felt about it is Threads. Built in pairs — a regime that existed, then the fiction that displaced it. The collection had already conceded the argument once without noticing: seven works of science fiction sit on its Cold War shelf, including one that is explicitly counterfactual, while ninety-five more sit unshelved.

17 sessions · 90 films placed · 6 terms

England by Class — a film syllabus

A third cut of the same shelf, asking who owns the ground and who works it. Built to show the collection's own lopsidedness rather than smooth it: against a canon of 67 titles, the satires of the ruling class are 7 of 11 held and the country house 5 of 9, while the British New Wave of 1958–63 — the decade English cinema discovered the working class — is 0 of 10. Session 16 therefore has no films in it on purpose. It is also the first home for 21 films no other syllabus had placed.

History of France — a film syllabus

Vercingetorix to the banlieue, across three terms. The deepest collection here, and the clearest demonstration that a national course is cross-shelf: fifty of the films it needs live under Ancient Rome, the World Wars, the Holocaust shelf and Africa.

History of Spain — a film syllabus

Al-Andalus to the present, in twenty-four sessions rather than thirty — the shelf is thinner and padding it would misrepresent it. The Reconquista and Golden Age are carried almost entirely by series, and every Conquest film sits on the MesoAmerica shelf.

History of India — a film syllabus

The Buddha to Mumbai in twenty-six sessions. Half of what the shelf holds was made by outsiders looking in — Kipling, frontier adventures, four Jungle Books — and the canon of Indian cinema is almost absent: no Apu Trilogy, no Mother India, no Sholay. The Mughals get one film. The gaps are named rather than papered over.

English Literature on Film — an extension

Shakespeare to Tolkien, following the books rather than the history. It exists because of a number: 585 films here are adaptations and 570 of them are not on the Literature shelf. Austen alone is scattered across seven shelves — no shelf can hold her, but a syllabus can.

Russian Literature on Film — an extension

The shortest module here, and deliberately so. The nineteenth-century Russian novel is among the most filmed bodies of literature anywhere, and this collection holds sixteen of those films against France's thirty-six. Dostoevsky has exactly one.

Russian Cinema — an extension

The optional side trip to the Russia course, on the model of an alumni travel brochure. The main course treats these films as evidence about the past; this one treats them as events. The complete montage school survives here — all five silents.

The American Presidency — a film syllabus

Washington to Trump in thirty-one sessions. Built from Wikidata ids rather than a tag, so the gaps are measured: thirty-five of the forty-five are depicted somewhere on the shelf, and seven of the eight who are not run consecutively from 1841 to 1861.

The Media and the Presidency — an extension

The press, the camera and the office. Opens in 1914, when Mutual Film Corporation paid Pancho Villa to let Hollywood film his revolution and D. W. Griffith produced the result a year before The Birth of a Nation.

11 sessions · 39 films · extends The American Presidency

The Invented Presidency — an extension

The presidents who never were, from a 1933 fantasy of benevolent dictatorship to the unnamed third-termer of Civil War. An invented president is where the office gets argued about rather than commemorated.

9 sessions · 31 films · extends The American Presidency

History through Music — a film syllabus

Twenty-three sessions on who paid the musician — church, court, patron, ticket-buyer, publisher, record label, and finally the scene itself. The hinge is 1927: before the phonograph the thing sold is a performance, after it the thing sold is a copy, and everything from ragtime to hip-hop follows from that change of noun. The second half is built to show the collection's own bias. The British Invasion is held eight ways and Motown — the American Black music it was covering — not once, so session 18 is nearly empty and the course ends in Detroit with a film about Eminem and none about Berry Gordy.

26 sessions · 114 films placed · 6 terms

Who Belongs — citizenship, migration and the state that decides

Twenty-three sessions on belonging as a legal status rather than a feeling — granted, withheld, inherited, forged or revoked by somebody with a desk. It opens with the reverse case: impressment, where a state refuses to let you resign a nationality. A boarding officer asks a sailor whether he was born in Liverpool, and “Aye, but I'm an American” is legally worthless. Then the waves — famine Irish, the Forty-Eighters, the shtetl, Ellis Island, the Chinese in the West — and the counter-movement that meets each one. It ends on the two modern reverse terms: the border that arrives while you stand still, and the status that goes provisional again after 2001.
